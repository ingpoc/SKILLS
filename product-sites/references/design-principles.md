## Taste thesis

Start with one point of view, not a catalog of fashionable techniques. A
strong product site can be described in one sentence and three to five axes.
Use fewer, stronger elements; let typography, proof, spacing, and sequencing
carry the design.

Default to edited, calm, and legible rather than empty. Negative space must
separate ideas, create emphasis, or set tempo. Space that only makes a section
feel expensive is decorative ink.

Prefer one signature move per page:

- an editorial scale shift;
- a system diagram that becomes product proof;
- a distinctive image crop;
- a precise timeline or ledger;
- a restrained paper, grid, or material texture;
- one meaningful transition between states.

## Layout and rhythm

Use one container model and a small number of deliberate exceptions. Align
headings, proof, and captions to shared gutters. Let major sections change
density and alignment without changing the underlying grid.

Use asymmetry to direct attention, not to manufacture novelty. A strong large
headline may counterbalance one proof surface; three unrelated floating
objects do not form a composition.

Keep each section's role singular:

- promise;
- orientation;
- mechanism;
- proof;
- trust;
- action.

Do not repeat the same centered heading plus three cards. Avoid nested cards
when an open band, list, table, rail, or figure communicates the relationship
more directly.

At common breakpoints, recompute the composition. Do not merely shrink it.
When a side-by-side proof becomes unreadable, stack labeled figures, crop to
the meaningful state, or split one composite into per-surface assets.

## Data-ink discipline

Treat every visible mark as either:

- information ink: product state, hierarchy, relationship, scale, action,
  provenance, or meaning;
- support ink: alignment, grouping, or navigation;
- decorative ink: atmosphere only.

Maximize useful ink by:

- direct-labeling evidence rather than adding distant legends;
- using position and typography before boxes and borders;
- removing frames that do not group anything;
- eliminating duplicate headings, labels, and explanatory copy;
- using one accent color for decisions, state, or sequence;
- letting real product content replace invented decoration.

Decorative ink is allowed when it establishes a coherent material character,
but it must never compete with proof. Faint grids, paper texture, large
watermarks, and rails fail when they reduce contrast, appear clipped, or imply
a relationship they do not encode.

## Typography and color

Choose a display voice and a utility voice. Make heading, body, label,
caption, and control roles explicit. Product-render text must remain legible at
the rendered CSS size; a beautiful screenshot with unreadable task rows is not
proof.

Lock the background temperature and palette from the approved concept. Do not
warm true white into cream or wash imagery with an unapproved overlay.

Use muted text carefully on textured or off-white backgrounds. Secondary copy
must remain comfortably readable; subordinate does not mean faint.

## Copy and evidence

Write specific customer language. Replace internal implementation tasks,
repository jargon, and developer-only status with actions a buyer or user can
understand.

Keep claims proportional to evidence:

- a real screenshot can prove an observed UI state;
- a timestamped paired capture can support continuity;
- a generated product render can explain intended experience;
- copy alone cannot prove synchronization, performance, or release readiness.

If the available image is clipped, noisy, or visibly unfinished, either obtain
a better real capture or create a polished product render. Label the latter
honestly and never use it as runtime acceptance evidence.

## Failure patterns

Reject:

- a hero that could belong to any SaaS product;
- a generic card wall;
- proof floating in a large empty poster;
- captions detached from their images;
- a composite raster that becomes unreadable on mobile;
- device screenshots used without explaining what to compare;
- a decorative timeline that encodes no progression;
- generated imagery presented as live proof;
- tiny gray support copy;
- a CTA delayed by a repetitive mobile sequence;
- motion that fades every section identically.
